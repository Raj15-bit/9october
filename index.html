2 wild days, and the thread that ties them

A crash and a rebound in Indian shares, and why the rising price of money sits behind both.

In short
  • Indian shares had their worst day since May, then their best day in 5 years.
  • The main weight on shares is the rising cost of borrowing across the world.
  • Oil, the US crackdown on work permits and doubts about AI money each added a push.

The common thread: the price of money

The common link across this issue is the cost of borrowing. US 30-year government debt was sold at the highest yield since 2000 (a yield is the yearly return a lender demands). When safe US debt pays this much, foreign money has less reason to sit in Indian shares, and the rupee weakens.

The AI boom feeds the same pressure. AI firms are borrowing record sums through special loan vehicles, and those loans compete with governments for the same savings. So when a report said OpenAI earns less than claimed, AI shares fell, US bond yields eased, and India opened higher the next morning. Section 09 follows that money.

  • Oil — tanker attacks spread beyond the Strait of Hormuz, and ship hire costs set records (section 03).
  • Rates at home — the RBI raised rates and tightened cash rules for banks (section 02).
  • Jobs abroad — the US barred Indian IT firms from a green-card route, yet their shares rose (section 06).
  • Policy relief — the GST Council removed arrest powers and widened tax credit (section 05).

The price of money keeps climbing

US and European borrowing costs at multi-decade highs, and the RBI reaching for a tool it last used in 2013.

In short
  • The US had to pay the most in 26 years to borrow long-term money.
  • Traders now expect the US central bank to raise rates again, not cut them.
  • In India, the RBI raised rates and told banks to keep almost all their required cash every day.

A 26-year high at the US debt sale

The US government sold $22bn of 30-year bonds, and buyers demanded a higher return than at any such sale since August 2000. Demand was not weak. Foreign and fund buyers took most of it, so the high yield reflects a new normal price for money, not a failed sale.

Have bonds peaked?

One view is that the pain is near its end. The Economist ran a cover asking "Will bonds blow up?", and covers like that often come near turning points; a chart strategist at BTIG called the 30-year move a "key reversal day". The opposite view is that nothing in the spending path changes, the US budget office expects all federal revenue to go on entitlements and interest by about 2030, and so yields stay high, with gold the better shelter.

A third point cuts across both. Big US tech shares have risen together with yields, not against them. So a fall in yields, if it came from AI doubts, might not lift tech shares at all.

India: a rate rise and a 2013 tool

The RBI raised its repo rate (the rate at which it lends to banks) to 5.50%, its first rise since 2023. It also made banks keep almost all of their required cash reserve (CRR) every single day, a rule it last tightened in 2013.

03

Oil, ships and war: the bill moves from the barrel to the boat and the refinery

Crude swings on every statement, but freight, insurance and refining are where the lasting costs now sit.

In short
  • Iran's navy now attacks tankers far beyond the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Hiring a supertanker costs over 10 times what it did a year ago.
  • The world is short of refineries, so diesel stays dear even if crude cools.

Mixed signals from Washington

Brent crude, the main world oil price, rose about 4% to $104.28 on 8 October. Then the US President posted that talks with Iran were "productive", that the US blockade stays, and that the US would not attack Iran before the midterm vote on 3 November. Oil dipped below $104. Hours later the US Treasury put fresh sanctions on Iran's shadow fleet (old tankers that move sanctioned oil).

Views split on what the no-attack pledge means. One view is that it buys time for talks, which mediators say are making progress. The opposite view is that it is a pre-election move to cool petrol prices, since reports say the Pentagon was asked for strike options before the vote, and Iran calls the talks stalled.

The war spreads along the sea lanes

  • Iran — its Revolutionary Guards say they will hit any ship using "illegal" routes anywhere in the region. At least 12 tankers were attacked in the week to 5 October, the most since the war began. On 9 October they set an LPG tanker on fire; another ship was hit 13 nautical miles west of Al Jazeera in the UAE.
  • Yemen — Houthi missiles hit Riyadh's King Khalid airport; Lufthansa and Air India suspended Riyadh flights. Houthis warned Saudi oil workers and reportedly laid mines in the Bab al-Mandeb strait. Betting markets put the chance of that strait being shut by year end at 17%.
  • Turkey — its foreign minister said the new Saudi-Turkey-Pakistan pact is for defence only; Turkey will help guard Saudi sites but will not attack Yemen. Turkey is also drilling for oil off Somalia.
  • Gulf of Aden — carries about 13% of world trade; Egypt's Suez Canal income has halved.
  • Russia — Ukrainian drones hit the Omsk refinery, Russia's largest, over 2,500 km from the border, and a Yandex data centre, the first known strike on Russian data sites.

Ships and insurers set the price now

Ship owners do not decide alone whether to sail. War-risk insurers set conditions on routes and guards, and a loaded supertanker is worth enough that the decision goes to the chief executive. So 1 attack a week is enough to keep the route's price high, whatever happens to crude.

  • US reserve — the emergency oil store held 284 million barrels in September, its lowest since 1982.
  • IMF — oil near $100 plus a refining margin of about another $100 a barrel gives record diesel; futures point to high prices through 2027.

How cheap is cheap?

The Nifty now trades below 19 times past profits. Large-company returns over the past 5 years are near their worst tenth on record, which in the past has come before better years.

  • Returns shrank — the 5-year yearly return fell 16.2 points for the Nifty 100, 19.2 for the Midcap 150 and 22.7 for the Smallcap 250.
  • Against bonds — Motilal Oswal says the earnings yield (profit divided by price) compared with the bond yield is at a 5-year high, and it expects 16% yearly profit growth over FY26-28.
  • Book value — the Nifty trades at 2.8 times book value; from similar low 5-year returns, the next 5 years have averaged about 15% a year.
  • Against US bonds — a share on 25 times profit earns 4% a year, less than the 5.25% on safe US debt, which is why foreigners see little reason to stay.

Is the low in?

One view is that the worst is close. CLSA's Vikash Jain says India is within days of an interim low and could rise 10-15%. HSBC also sees a possible turn.

The opposite view is that the bounce is a trap. Fewer than 1 in 5 shares trade above their 20-day average, small and micro-cap shares are breaking down, and the rally was led by beaten-down IT and consumer names. A sharper bear case says the market peaked long ago: foreigners, private equity and promoters sell by choice, while Indian funds buy only because monthly savings keep arriving.

  • Banks — Indian banks have lagged rich-country banks by about 100 percentage points in 3 years with similar returns on equity. The catch: foreign funds keep selling the big private banks, which suggests they think the discount is deserved.

The CLSA call comes with its own test: it is wrong if US 10-year yields reach 6% or Indian ones 8%. So the bond market in section 02, not company news, will likely settle this.

Froth in small shares

DSP warns that small and mid-sized shares are dear against large ones. The ratio of the small-cap index to the Sensex is about 72% above its usual level, the highest in over 20 years.

  • History — after past peaks in this ratio (2007, 2010, 2017), small caps lagged large caps by about 30% over the next 2 years. DSP prefers large caps now.
  • Cupid — up about 240% in 2026 to a Rs50,000 crore market value on sales under Rs500 crore, or over 350 times profit.
  • Shanti Gold International — up about 112% in 6 months to a new high.
  • PNGS Reva Diamond Jewellery — up about 81% in 6 months.
  • Governance — several shares with weak governance records gave big returns, a reminder to size such bets small.

Reading between the lines, the market is paying up for the few names with rising profits and dumping the rest, whatever the price. Cheap large shares keep falling while dear small ones keep rising, which is how a market looks late in a fall, not early in a rally.

India's economy: easier tax rules, dearer food, a dry year

The GST reset, trade, the weak monsoon, what families pay, and the fight over who connects India from space.

Reading between the lines, drug makers' own selling prices are untouched. The losers are distributors, chemists and hospital pharmacies, which earn a large share of their profit on these markups.

  • China too — Alibaba, Tencent and Baidu together now burn cash, as China's AI spending doubles to about ¥932bn this year.

Views split. One view, from Goldman Sachs, is that the AI boom has become a cost-of-money problem: it competes with governments and other firms for the same savings, which pushes yields up (section 02). The opposite view is starker: a well-known crypto investor calls it the biggest bubble of his lifetime, and SocGen's Albert Edwards notes that productivity is not rising despite the spending, as in Thailand before 1997.

Reading between the lines, each step down the ladder (shares, then loans, then junk bonds, then Gulf money) is a sign that cheaper sources are used up. That pattern usually shows up late in a boom, not early.
